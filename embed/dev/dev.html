Embed harness

Renders the built embed against dev.json, a fixture snapshot — no Appwrite, no login, no publish. Source lives in embed/dev/ and is copied into the build output by Vite’s publicDir, so it never reaches a production deploy.

Run npm run build:embed, start the dev server, then open /embed/dev.html.

Checklist: pins coloured by their first tag (untagged ones grey), the four locations with an icon drawn as large balls with a white glyph and the rest as small ones, every pin centred on its own coordinate, the pair beside the flagship clusters at low zoom (mixing both sizes into one bubble), clicking either one opens a popup clear of the pin and clear of the results panel, search narrows by name or address, a no-match search says so, and OpenStreetMap attribution shows exactly once. Set allowedDomains to something other than localhost and the map should refuse to render, warning to the console only.

The card is never under the panel, and never cut off. With the panel floating on the right a card opens to the left of its pin and the other way round on the left, because the placement measures the room the panel leaves rather than the whole map frame; docked or switched off, it lands where it always did. And the card is exactly as wide as the popup that holds it — MapLibre’s cap counts its own tip, so a 320px card in a 320px popup used to lose ~10px off its edge and jump sideways by 4px whenever anything on it repainted. Hover the gallery arrows and nothing on the card may move.

Every tag label is searchable: typing depots must narrow the map to those two locations even though Depots is a chip you could have pressed instead. That matters because a long filter row scrolls, and a tag a visitor never scrolled to is one they can still type.

The pin, the card and the row agree on one colour. A location wears several tags and its pin can only be one, so the first tag in the fixture’s own order is the colour: the flagship is shops first, so it is orange, and the chip carrying the dot on its card is Shops — not Bikes, which comes first in the map’s vocabulary. If the dot ever moves to a different chip from the pin’s colour, `tagChipsOf` has been sorted somewhere it must not be.

/embed/dev-legacy.html renders the same bundle against a snapshot published before the merge — the §7 check. Run it after touching anything in this list.

The card: a photo, then the name, address, description, tags and the week, then two buttons and the links row. The fixture carries a whole cardLayout of its own, so this is a card as published — the embed draws it without re-resolving anything, which is the path a customer’s site takes. /embed/dev-legacy.html has no cardLayout at all and is where the defaultCardLayout() fallback still gets exercised. Open a pin near the very top or the very bottom of the frame — the card must pan itself fully into view rather than being clipped, and the map must move once, not twice.

Buttons. Two of them, side by side at 50% each: a blue pill reading “Book a fitting” and an outlined one reading “Take me there”. The first is in link mode pointed at the booking field, so it appears on the flagship and nowhere else — every other location left that field blank, and a button with nothing to point at draws nothing and takes no space. The second is in directions mode, which is what a button says by carrying no action at all, so it is on every card; its href must be a real google.com/maps/dir URL built from that pin’s own coordinates (an iPhone gets maps.apple.com instead). Neither opens in this tab. The blue one takes its colour, its 20px corner and its bold label from the block; the outlined one has no background of its own, so it must wear the stylesheet’s ground rather than a colour baked into the fixture — that is the §7 promise the whole model rests on.

The links row leaves Directions out (hideDirections: true), which is the reason those checkboxes exist: the button above it is already the way there, and the row would otherwise ask the same question twice. So the flagship’s row shows Phone, the site and the “Book a fitting” CTA and no Directions link. Old town corner shows its email alone. A row with all four unticked must vanish entirely rather than leave an empty box with its own padding in it.

Every row in it is a glyph and a label, at the card’s own small-print size and colour — not a blue link, and not the word “Website”: a site reads as its host and path (example.com/gedimino), which is what the card tool draws. The two renderers are twins, so anything that looks different here from the same card in /maps/…/card is a bug in one of them.

Gallery: the flagship carries three pictures with ‹ › arrows that wrap in both directions; the arrows must not pan the map underneath. There is no counter over the picture — a visitor looking at a shop’s photos is not counting them, and the card tool has never drawn one. The Naujamiestis depot has one photo written the old way (photoUrl with no photoUrls) and must show it with no arrows at all — that is every snapshot published before galleries existed.

Shapes: a blue circle around the flagship and an orange polygon over the old town, both drawn under every pin and cluster, both keeping their outline solid while the fill stays translucent. The circle must hold its ground distance across zoom levels — pinch in and out and it should cover the same streets, not the same number of pixels. Clicking a fill opens its card where you clicked; clicking a pin that sits inside a shape must still open the pin's card, not the shape's — the flagship sits at the circle's own centre, so it is the one to try. Clicking the route line opens its card with its length and its drive on it — “1.5 km · 19 min”. The length is summed from the points in the snapshot; the time is the one figure that travels with them, because no arrangement of coordinates says how fast you may drive along them. A line with no durationS must show the distance alone, exactly as it did before routes existed.

Results list: a panel of locations beside the map, with search and the filters docked at the top of it. Clicking a row opens that location's card and marks the row; clicking a pin scrolls its row into view and marks it. Narrow the browser window and nothing should change — the breakpoints are container queries, so squeeze the map's own box instead and it should stack with the map on top. The map takes 60% of the box and the panel 40%, at both layouts. “Nearest to me” is a crosshair button — hover it for the name, and it must re-sort the whole list by distance rather than jumping to one pin.

The crosshair lights while distances are being measured, and pressing it lit stops measuring and puts the owner’s own order back. There is no Near you · Clear chip under the toolbar any more: it was a line of text and a second control explaining one button. Picking a place out of the search box lights it too — the distances are measured from somewhere either way.

A Directions press must open instantly, and must never open a blank tab. This is the one that has been broken twice. Nothing in the embed may intercept the click: no window.open, no preventDefault. The first press does start a location lookup behind it — which may raise the permission prompt — but the tab is Google’s the whole time, and pressing a second link afterwards must raise no second prompt.

One beacon per session, and not one before the tab goes away. This fixture carries an analytics block, so open the Network tab and click a pin, type in the search box and press Directions: there must be no request to /api/collect while you do it. Then switch to another tab — exactly one POST appears, and it answers 204. A second flush must never follow it. The fixture’s map id is not a real one, so the collector drops the row on purpose; 204 is the right answer either way, because a stranger’s page must learn nothing from us. The URL is hard-coded to port 3000 — on another port the request fails, which is the harness being wrong rather than the embed. So what this page proves is that the embed sends correctly, never that anything was stored — those are different halves and a 204 looks identical for both. For the other half use /embed/live.html, which points the same bundle at a real published snapshot and does write rows.

Whatever the browser says is what gets sent. Every Directions link — the rows, the card’s links row and a Button block left on its default action — carries an &origin= as soon as anything knows where you are, however vague. There used to be a 50-metre bar here on the theory that Google does better unaided; it does not, unless Google has been given a location of its own, and dropping a coarse fix turned a route that started on the wrong street into one that started in another town.

The sharpest reading wins, not the latest. Press the crosshair (a cheap cached fix) and then the map’s own Find my location (a precise one) and the links must move to the precise one — then press the crosshair again and they must not move back. The accuracy circle that control draws is the radius the browser is claiming, and it is the same number a route starts from: what you see as the circle is the honest picture of how well this can possibly work.

Location refused: block this site's location in the browser's own site settings, then press the crosshair. The button must disable itself while it asks, the pill must say Location is off for this site rather than a generic failure, and it must disappear on its own after a few seconds — a click takes it away sooner. The pill is the one thing here that cannot be checked by looking at a working map: it used to have no way of clearing at all on a snapshot published without search and without filters.

Tags: three filter groups — “Sells”, “Open” and “Categories”, the last being what a migrated map looks like — each under its own heading. Within a group the chips widen the results — ticking Bikes and Skis shows more shops than Bikes alone — and across groups they narrow: Bikes plus Sundays leaves only the flagship. That asymmetry is the whole rule; if ticking a second chip in one group ever removes a location, the matching is wrong.

Custom fields: the flagship's card carries a “Book a fitting” CTA inside the links row — the small one, not the pill above it, which is a Button block pointed at the same field — plus “Dealer code” and “Workshop” as labelled rows — in the order the fixture defines them, not the order the values sit in. The button shows the owner's label, never the URL. Old town corner has only a dealer code, and the two locations with no values must show no field rows at all.

Search: typing filters the locations immediately, and after a beat offers Lithuanian places and postcodes underneath — run npm run build:gazetteer -- LT first, or the list stays empty and the console says why. Pick “Kaunas”, which has no location in it, and the map should fly there while the list re-sorts by distance from Kaunas rather than emptying. A chip under the controls says where the distances are measured from, and clears it. The whole control works from the keyboard: type, arrow down, Enter, Escape.

Appearance: the fixture sets labels: "some" and turns points of interest off and cycle paths on, so the basemap should carry city and district names but no street names, water names or shop markers, and dedicated cycleways should be drawn as green ribbons under the labels. All of it is applied to the style before the map is built — switch your OS to dark and reload, and the same three should still hold on top of the inverted basemap, because autoDark composes with them rather than replacing them.
